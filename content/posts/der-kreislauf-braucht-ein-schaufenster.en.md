When people talk about the circular economy, most think of the workshop: the laptop that gets opened up, cleaned, and brought back to life. That is the visible, likeable half — and it matters. But it is only half. A device that sits repaired on a shelf is not saved yet. It is merely postponed e-waste with better prospects.

The loop does not close in the workshop. It closes the moment someone uses the device again.

## A device is only saved once it is working again

The maths behind reuse is simple: a laptop that gets a second life is a laptop that does not have to be mined, manufactured, and shipped halfway around the world a second time. Most of a computer's ecological footprint is created in production, not in use — which is why every additional year of use counts. And we show our working: every CO₂ figure on our platform is tied to open sources, with the methodology [publicly documented](/transparenz/co2). Substantiated, not asserted.

But that maths only works out if the device actually finds its way to a person who needs it. And this is where circular economy efforts fail most often — not at repairing, but at passing on. The repaired device needs a shop window: a place where it can be found, understood, and bought. Distribution is not a dirty word. It is the missing half of the loop.

## What a shop window has to deliver

A shop window for used technology only works on trust. Whoever buys a new device relies on the brand. Whoever buys a used one has to be able to rely on the person offering it. That is why a few principles hold in the [evig marketplace](/marketplace):

- **Complete, working machines — no tinkering projects.** Buying from us never means "spares donor, condition unknown". If it is in the window, it runs.
- **Honest claims instead of hopeful guesses.** A device is good for what we tested and set it up for — not for what its spec sheet once promised.
- **Transparent impact.** The CO₂ saving on every listing has an open derivation, not a fantasy number.
- **One window for everyone.** Alongside our refurbished machines, private individuals can pass on their devices too. Every reuse counts — including person to person. The loop does not belong to us; we just put up the shop window for it.

Around the purchase sits everything that turns it into lasting use: [repair and IT help](/it-hilfe) when something jams, and [workshops](/workshops) so the knowledge travels with the machine. Hardware without knowledge is a paperweight factory; we hand over both together.

## The cheapest on-ramp to intelligence

There is one more reason this shop window matters to us — and it reaches beyond ecology.

A well-refurbished laptop is, today, the cheapest on-ramp to artificial intelligence there is. The same machine that would have ended up in a cupboard or a container is — set up right — a doorway to the most powerful tools ever built. Whoever wants to learn, work, found something, or simply keep up does not need a new two-thousand-franc computer. They need a reliable device, honestly set up, at a price they can actually reach.

For us, circular economy and access to intelligence are neither a trade-off nor two separate projects. They are the same act, seen from two sides: keeping a device in the loop *is* the cheapest way to give someone access.

## What this means in practice

The loop does not need a guilty conscience; it needs paths. Three of them are open:

1. **Find a device.** Browse the [marketplace](/marketplace) — the machine that makes a new purchase unnecessary might already be there.
2. **Pass a device on.** What sits unused at your place can be working at someone else's. List it as a private ad — the shop window is ready.
3. **Keep a device alive.** Before replacing something: [get it repaired](/it-hilfe). Every repair is one device fewer in the bin.

Reuse, to us, is neither a duty nor a sacrifice. It is energy: *this still has a life in it — let's bring it out.* The workshop makes devices usable again. The shop window makes them used again. Only together do they make a circular economy.
